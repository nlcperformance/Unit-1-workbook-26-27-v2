Learning Aim A: The Skeletal System

Specification Coverage: A1, A2, A3, A4, A5, A6

A1–A6

Key Technical Terms & Examiner Vocabulary

Anatomy & Physiology Anatomy is the study of structure such as the skeletal system; Physiology is the study of the way the body responds to exercise[cite: 1].
Axial Skeleton (80 Bones) Maintains central axis: Skull (cranium/facial bones), Thoracic cage (sternum/ribs), Vertebral Column[cite: 1].
Appendicular Skeleton (126 Bones) Facilitates movement: Shoulder girdle, Pelvic girdle, Upper limbs, and Lower limbs[cite: 1].
Osteoblasts & Osteoclasts Osteoblasts bring calcium to bones to create bone matter[cite: 1]; Osteoclasts reabsorb unnecessary calcium[cite: 1].
Epiphyseal Plate Growing areas at the ends of long bones that allow the bone to grow longer during childhood[cite: 1].
Articulation A joint formed where two or more bones meet[cite: 1].
Synovial Fluid Viscous liquid secreted by the synovial membrane to lubricate joints and reduce friction between bones[cite: 1].
Bone Mineral Density (BMD) Mineral concentration elevated via weight-bearing activity, making bones stronger and denser[cite: 1].

A1 & A3 Content

Vertebral Column Regions & Postural Deviations

33 Vertebrae Across 5 Spine Regions:

Region Number Anatomical Role & Features[cite: 1]
Cervical 7 (C1–C7)[cite: 1] The neck vertebrae; C1 (atlas) & C2 (axis) form a pivot joint allowing the head and neck to move freely[cite: 1]. Smallest and most vulnerable vertebrae[cite: 1].
Thoracic 12 (T1–T12)[cite: 1] Mid-spine vertebrae which articulate with the ribs to form a dome-shaped structure protecting the heart and lungs[cite: 1].
Lumbar 5 (L1–L5)[cite: 1] Lower back; the largest movable vertebrae that support the most weight and provide attachment for back muscles[cite: 1]. Discs produce a concave curve[cite: 1].
Sacral 5 (Fused)[cite: 1] Fused into a triangular bone (sacrum) forming the back wall of the pelvic girdle between the hip bones[cite: 1].
Coccygeal 4 (Fused)[cite: 1] Bottom of the column fused to form the coccyx or tail bone[cite: 1].

Postural Deviations & Neutral Spine:

Neutral Spine: Good posture with correct position of the three natural curves in cervical, thoracic, and lumbar regions when viewed from the side[cite: 1]. Completely vertical from the front[cite: 1].
Kyphosis: Excessive outward curve of the thoracic region resulting in a 'hunchback' appearance[cite: 1]. Often caused by poor posture or deformities of the vertebrae[cite: 1].
Scoliosis: Abnormal lateral (left or right) curvature of the spine, mostly occurring in the thoracic region[cite: 1]. Not thought to be linked to bad posture and seems to be inheritable[cite: 1].
A2 & A3 Content

Functions of the Skeleton & Synovial Joint Classification

1. Support: Provides supporting framework for the soft tissues of your body and gives body shape[cite: 1].
2. Protection: Surrounds and protects vital tissues (e.g. skull protects brain, thorax protects heart and lungs)[cite: 1].
3. Leverage: Provides a lever system against which muscles can pull to create movement[cite: 1].
4. Mineral Storage: Bone acts as a reservoir for minerals such as calcium and phosphorus[cite: 1].
5. Blood Cell Production: Red and white blood cells are continually produced in the bone marrow[cite: 1].
6. Weight Bearing: Very strong bones support weight of tissue and provide structural strength to prevent injury during large sport forces[cite: 1].
7. Muscle Attachment: Skeleton parts provide a surface for skeletal muscles to attach via tendons[cite: 1].
8. Friction Reduction: Synovial joints secrete fluid that prevents bones rubbing together, reducing friction[cite: 1].

Synovial Joint Structural Features:

Articular Cartilage: Smooth and slippery covering on bone ends that stops bones rubbing or grinding together with minimum friction[cite: 1].

Joint Capsule: Outer sleeve fibrous capsule holding bones in place and protecting the joint[cite: 1].

Synovial Membrane: Capsule lining that secretes synovial fluid[cite: 1].

Synovial Fluid: Viscous liquid lubricating the joint, reducing friction, and providing nutrients to articular cartilage[cite: 1].

Bursa: Small fluid-filled sac providing a cushion between tendons and bones, preventing friction[cite: 1].

Ligaments: Tough bands of connective tissue holding bones together to avoid dislocation while remaining flexible[cite: 1].

Joint Type Anatomical Location Permitted Movements[cite: 1]
Hinge Elbow, Knee Movement in one direction only (forwards and backwards)[cite: 1]
Ball & Socket Shoulder, Hip Round end fits into cup-shaped socket; allows movement in all directions[cite: 1]
Pivot Atlas/Axis (Neck) Circular bone fits over a peg allowing controlled rotational movement[cite: 1]
Condyloid Wrist Bump sits in hollow; backwards, forwards, side to side (rotation prevented by ligaments)[cite: 1]
Gliding Carpals, Tarsals Movement over flat surface in all directions, restricted by ligaments[cite: 1]
Saddle Base of Thumb Concave/convex surfaces; backwards, forwards, and side to side[cite: 1]
A4, A5 & A6 Content

Acute Responses, Chronic Adaptations & Health Conditions

Acute Responses (Short-Term)
  • Producing more synovial fluid to lubricate joints and protect bones during increased demand[cite: 1].
  • Synovial fluid becomes less viscous (thinner), increasing movement fluidity and joint range of movement[cite: 1].
  • Release of synovial fluid provides increased nutrients to articular cartilage[cite: 1].
  • Increased uptake of minerals (such as calcium) within the bones to cope with exercise demand[cite: 1].
Chronic Adaptations (Long-Term)
  • Increased Bone Mineral Density (BMD) making bones stronger and more resistant to forces like kicking or running[cite: 1].
  • Increased strength of ligaments that attach bones at synovial joints[cite: 1].
  • Increased ligament pliability leading to enhanced joint flexibility over time[cite: 1].
  • Thickening of cartilage tissue[cite: 1].
Additional Factors & Health
  • Osteoarthritis: Inflammation caused by general wear and tear reducing cartilage tissue, resulting in bones rubbing together[cite: 1]. Prevented by regular exercise improving lubrication[cite: 1].
  • Osteoporosis: Weakening of bones caused by calcium/vitamin D loss, making bones brittle[cite: 1]. High-impact / resistance training increases bone density[cite: 1].
  • Age: Children's bones are still growing; heavy resistance training can damage epiphyseal plates resulting in stunted growth[cite: 1].

Unit 1: Anatomy & Physiology — Master Learner Workbook Learning Aim B: The Muscular System

Learning Aim B: The Muscular System

Specification Coverage: B1, B2, B3, B4, B5, B6, B7, B8

B1–B8

Key Technical Terms & Examiner Vocabulary

Agonist (Prime Mover) Muscle principally responsible for the movement taking place; the contracting muscle that shortens to move a joint[cite: 1].
Antagonist Muscle relaxing in opposition to the agonist to control and brake the movement[cite: 1].
Synergist & Fixator Synergist modifies pull direction to help agonist[cite: 1]; Fixator stabilizes the origin bone so the agonist can effectively contract[cite: 1].
Type I (Slow Oxidative) Slow-twitch fibres suited to longer-duration aerobic activities due to high fatigue resistance, rich blood supply, and many mitochondria[cite: 1].
Type IIa (Fast Oxidative) Fast-contracting fibres able to produce great force while being resistant to fatigue, suited to 400m runs[cite: 1].
Type IIx (Fast Glycolytic) Rapid contracting fibres for large force production that fatigue readily, reliant entirely on anaerobic respiration[cite: 1].
All-or-None Law Muscle fibres within a motor unit either respond completely or not at all to a sufficient nerve impulse stimulus[cite: 1].
Hypertrophy vs Sarcopenia Hypertrophy is muscle fibre enlargement from protein increases[cite: 1]; Sarcopenia is the age-related decrease in muscle mass[cite: 1].
B1, B2 & B3 Content

Muscle Tissue Types & Contraction Mechanics

Skeletal Muscle Voluntary control (under conscious control) and striated appearance[cite: 1]. Attached to bones via tendons, primarily responsible for movement and generating heat[cite: 1].
Smooth Muscle Involuntary muscle working without conscious thought[cite: 1]. Located in blood vessel walls and digestive system to regulate digestion and blood pressure[cite: 1].
Cardiac Muscle Involuntary muscle found exclusively in the heart wall[cite: 1]. Specialised striated tissue that works continuously without fatiguing to pump blood[cite: 1].

Types of Muscle Contraction:

Concentric (Isotonic) Muscle shortens to move a joint[cite: 1]. Known as the positive phase[cite: 1].
Example: Upward phase of bicep curl where brachialis and bicep shorten[cite: 1].
Eccentric (Isotonic) Muscle returns to its normal length after shortening against resistance, acting as a braking mechanism[cite: 1].
Example: Controlled lowering of arm in bicep curl, or quadriceps controlling downhill running[cite: 1].
Isometric Muscle length does not change and joint angle does not alter, though muscle actively holds a static position[cite: 1].
Example: Abdominal plank position or holding a rugby scrum[cite: 1].
B5 Content

Muscle Fibre Type Comparison Table

Feature Type I (Slow-Twitch)[cite: 1] Type IIa (Fast-Oxidative)[cite: 1] Type IIx (Fast-Glycolytic)[cite: 1]
Contraction Speed & Force Contract slowly with less force[cite: 1] Fast-contracting producing great force[cite: 1] Contract rapidly producing large amounts of force[cite: 1]
Fatigue Resistance Slow to fatigue[cite: 1] Resistant to fatigue but less than Type I[cite: 1] Fatigue more readily[cite: 1]
Capillary Supply & Respiration Rich blood supply for aerobic metabolism[cite: 1] Less reliant on oxygen than slow-twitch[cite: 1] Depend almost entirely on anaerobic respiration[cite: 1]
Mitochondrial Density Contain many mitochondria[cite: 1] Moderate/Medium Low
Sporting Example Long-distance running and swimming[cite: 1] 400m running, weight training (10-12 reps)[cite: 1] Stop-go sports (rugby/football), short sprints[cite: 1]
B6, B7 & B8 Content

Exercise Responses, Chronic Hypertrophy & Additional Factors

Acute Responses (Short-Term)
  • Vasodilation increases blood supply to meet oxygen and glucose demands[cite: 1].
  • Increased muscle temperature produced as a waste product of energy reactions, making muscles more pliable and flexible[cite: 1].
  • Micro-tears in muscle fibres during resistance stress causing swelling[cite: 1].
  • Lactic acid accumulation during high-intensity anaerobic exercise causing burning sensation[cite: 1].
Chronic Adaptations (Long-Term)
  • Hypertrophy: Muscle fibres become larger due to protein increases[cite: 1].
  • Increased size and number of mitochondria improving aerobic performance[cite: 1].
  • Increased myoglobin stores enabling greater oxygen transport to mitochondria[cite: 1].
  • Increased glycogen storage, enabling higher intensity work for longer periods[cite: 1].
  • Increased tolerance to lactic acid via extended capillary networks[cite: 1].
  • Increased tendon strength and cartilage thickening[cite: 1].
Additional Factors
  • Cramp: Sudden involuntary contraction often caused by dehydration resulting in inadequate oxygen and mineral supply[cite: 1].
  • DOMS: Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness felt 24-48 hours post-exercise due to microtears, primarily from eccentric contractions[cite: 1].
  • Sarcopenia: Age-related decrease in muscle mass starting around age 50[cite: 1].

Unit 1: Anatomy & Physiology — Master Learner Workbook Learning Aim C: The Respiratory System

Learning Aim C: The Respiratory System

Specification Coverage: C1, C2, C3, C4, C5, C6, C7

C1–C7

Key Technical Terms & Examiner Vocabulary

Tidal Volume Volume of air breathed in and out with each breath (~500ml at rest)[cite: 1].
Minute Volume Total air volume passing through the lungs per minute; determined by breathing rate x tidal volume[cite: 1].
Diffusion / Gaseous Exchange Gas exchange across the respiratory membrane from an area of high concentration to lower concentration[cite: 1]. Oxygen diffuses into blood, carbon dioxide diffuses into alveoli[cite: 1].
Medulla Oblongata Location of dorsal and ventral respiratory groups in the brain stem that control the active process of inspiration[cite: 1].
Chemoreceptors Sensory receptors in carotid arteries and aortic arch detecting changing blood carbon dioxide and acidity levels to alter breathing rates[cite: 1].
Residual Volume The air remaining in lungs after maximal expiration (~1200ml) preventing lungs from collapsing[cite: 1].
Vital Capacity Maximum air volume forced out of the lungs after maximal inspiration (~4800ml)[cite: 1].
Inspiratory Reserve Volume Additional volume of fresh air taken in beyond normal tidal volume by breathing deeply (~3000ml)[cite: 1].
C1 & C2 Content

Anatomical Conducting Zone & Mechanics of Breathing

Conducting & Respiratory Zone Pathway:

1. Nasal Cavity: Hairs filter out dust and pollen; air is warmed and moistened[cite: 1].
2. Pharynx & Larynx: Pharynx is a skeletal muscle funnel; Larynx (voice box) has rigid muscle/cartilage walls and connects to trachea[cite: 1].
3. Trachea: Windpipe with C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings to prevent collapse[cite: 1].
4. Bronchi & Bronchioles: Trachea branches into left/right bronchi, dividing into 23 orders of smaller bronchioles lacking cartilage[cite: 1].
5. Alveoli: 300 million microscopic air sacs providing massive surface area for gaseous exchange[cite: 1].

Mechanics of Inspiration vs Expiration:

Action Inspiration (Inhalation)[cite: 1] Expiration (Exhalation)[cite: 1]
Diaphragm Forced downwards (contracts)[cite: 1] Relaxes and moves upwards[cite: 1]
Intercostal Muscles External intercostals contract to lift ribs upwards and outwards[cite: 1] Intercostal muscles relax moving ribs downwards and inwards[cite: 1]
Thoracic Volume Expansion of thorax in all directions[cite: 1] Volume decreases[cite: 1]
Pressure Drops below atmospheric pressure encouraging air in[cite: 1] Increases pushing air out[cite: 1]
C3 & C4 Content

Spirometry Lung Volumes & Gaseous Exchange Pathways

Minute Ventilation Equation:

$$V_e = \text{Tidal Volume (TV)} \times \text{Breathing Rate (BR)}$$

Resting Example: $500\,\text{ml} \times 12\,\text{breaths/min} = 6,000\,\text{ml/min}\ (6\,\text{L/min})$[cite: 1]

During strenuous exercise, oxygen diffusion increases and minute ventilation can reach ~15 times resting values[cite: 1].

Gaseous Exchange Diffusion:

Oxygen: Alveolar blood has higher oxygen concentration than capillary blood from pulmonary arteries; oxygen diffuses through thin capillary walls and red cell membranes to latch onto haemoglobin[cite: 1].

Carbon Dioxide: Deoxygenated blood has higher carbon dioxide concentration; it diffuses from blood plasma into alveoli for expiration[cite: 1].

C5, C6 & C7 Content

Exercise Responses, Chronic Adaptations & Environmental Factors

Acute Responses (Short-Term)
  • Anticipatory rise in breathing rate prior to exercise[cite: 1].
  • Increased rate and depth of breathing due to higher carbon dioxide production[cite: 1].
  • Increased tidal volume triggered by oxygen depletion[cite: 1].
  • Capillary network surrounding alveoli expands, increasing blood flow and pulmonary diffusion[cite: 1].
Chronic Adaptations (Long-Term)
  • Increased vital capacity for more efficient oxygen supply to muscles[cite: 1].
  • Increased strength of respiratory muscles (diaphragm and intercostals) allowing greater chest cavity expansion[cite: 1].
  • Increased oxygen and carbon dioxide diffusion rate allowing harder, longer training[cite: 1].
Additional Factors
  • Asthma: Bands of muscle surrounding airways contract and tighten restricting air movement; managed by preventer and reliever inhalers (e.g., Paula Radcliffe)[cite: 1].
  • Altitude: Lower air pressure reduces oxygen density causing hypoxia; lungs acclimatise over time by becoming larger and body produces more red blood cells[cite: 1].

Unit 1: Anatomy & Physiology — Master Learner Workbook Learning Aim D: The Cardiovascular System

Learning Aim D: The Cardiovascular System

Specification Coverage: D1, D2, D3, D4, D5, D6

D1–D6

Key Technical Terms & Examiner Vocabulary

Sinoatrial Node (SAN) Heart's primary pacemaker in the right atrium wall sending impulse through atrial walls causing systole[cite: 1].
Atrioventricular Node (AVN) Acts as a buffer slowing down the signal from SAN by ~0.1s allowing atria to empty into ventricles[cite: 1].
Cardiac Output ($Q$) Amount of blood pumped out of the left ventricle in one minute ($Q = SV \times HR$)[cite: 1].
Vascular Shunt Mechanism (Thermoregulation) Redistribution of blood flow during exercise via vasodilation (increases vessel diameter) to skin/muscles and vasoconstriction (decreases diameter) away from internal organs[cite: 1].
Blood Pressure (Systolic/Diastolic) Systolic is pressure exerted when heart contracts; Diastolic is pressure when heart relaxes between beats[cite: 1].
Cardiovascular Drift Not explicitly in Unit 1 excerpt provided, but relates to reduced SV and increased HR in heat (extrapolated from syllabus).
Bradycardia & Hypertrophy Cardiac hypertrophy thickens left ventricle wall; bradycardia is the resulting decrease in resting heart rate as SV rises[cite: 1].
Erythrocytes & Leucocytes Red blood cells containing haemoglobin to carry oxygen; White blood cells identifying and destroying pathogens[cite: 1].
D1 & D2 Content

Cardiac Anatomy, Vessels & Conduction

Heart Structure:

Heart Wall: Epicardium (outer), Myocardium (strong middle muscle), Endocardium (inner)[cite: 1].
Chambers: Atria receive blood; Ventricles (thicker walls) pump blood out (right to lungs, left to body)[cite: 1].
Valves: Bicuspid (left), Tricuspid (right) prevent backflow into atria; Semi-lunar valves (aortic/pulmonary) prevent backflow into ventricles[cite: 1].
Vessels: Aorta carries oxygenated blood to body; Vena Cava returns deoxygenated blood to right atrium; Pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated to lungs; Pulmonary vein returns oxygenated to left atrium[cite: 1].

Vessel Comparison:

Arteries[cite: 1] Veins[cite: 1]
Carry blood away from heart Carry blood to the heart
Found deeper in body; thick muscular walls Just beneath skin; thinner walls
Blood under high pressure; no valves Low pressure; contain pocket valves to prevent backflow
D3, D4, D5 & D6 Content

Exercise Responses, Chronic Hypertrophy & Additional Factors

Acute Responses (Short-Term)
  • Anticipatory increase in heart rate prior to exercise via nerves/chemicals[cite: 1].
  • Increased heart rate and pumping strength driven by brain nerve centres detecting activity[cite: 1].
  • Increased cardiac output primarily through elevated heart rate as stroke volume limits are reached[cite: 1].
  • Increased systolic blood pressure as heart forces more oxygenated blood to muscles[cite: 1].
  • Redirection of blood flow via vasodilation and vasoconstriction (Thermoregulation)[cite: 1].
Chronic Adaptations (Long-Term)
  • Cardiac Hypertrophy (thickening of left ventricle wall increasing contraction strength)[cite: 1].
  • Increase in resting and exercising stroke volume reducing workload on the heart[cite: 1].
  • Decrease in resting heart rate and reduction in resting blood pressure[cite: 1].
  • Capillarisation of skeletal/cardiac muscle and increased blood volume[cite: 1].
  • Decreased heart rate recovery time[cite: 1].
Additional Factors
  • SADS: Sudden Arrhythmic Death Syndrome - genetic condition disrupting normal heart rhythm (e.g. Fabrice Muamba)[cite: 1].
  • Blood Pressure Issues: Hypertension (danger during sudden HR increases) and Hypotension (slow blood flow restricting oxygen delivery)[cite: 1].
  • Temperature: Hyperthermia (heat trapped in body) and Hypothermia (core drops below 35°C)[cite: 1].

Unit 1: Anatomy & Physiology — Master Learner Workbook Learning Aim E: Energy Systems

Learning Aim E: Energy Systems

Specification Coverage: E1, E2, E3, E4, E5, E6

E1–E6

Key Technical Terms & Examiner Vocabulary

ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate) Molecule storing chemical energy; formed by binding ADP with a phosphate group[cite: 1]. Energy released when bond is broken[cite: 1].
Phosphocreatine (PC) High-energy phosphate compound stored in muscles used to rapidly resynthesise ATP by transferring its energy to ADP[cite: 1].
Anaerobic Glycolysis Partial breakdown of glucose and glycogen without oxygen to resynthesise ATP; produces 2 ATP (glucose) or 3 ATP (glycogen)[cite: 1].
Lactic Acid Limiting by-product of anaerobic glycolysis that diffuses into tissue fluid and impedes muscle contraction[cite: 1].
Krebs Cycle Second phase of aerobic respiration in mitochondria; converts pyruvic acid to citric acid producing 2 ATP, $CO_2$, and hydrogen[cite: 1].
Electron Transport Chain Most important aerobic step in mitochondria producing 34 ATP molecules from glucose by combining hydrogen and oxygen[cite: 1].
Hypoglycaemia Abnormally low level of blood glucose causing trembling, sweating, confusion, and potential unconsciousness[cite: 1].
Diabetes (Type I) Condition where blood glucose is too high because insulin cannot properly facilitate glucose entry into body cells[cite: 1].
E1 – E4 Content

Energy Systems Master Comparison Table

System Fuel Source & Traits[cite: 1] Duration & Yield[cite: 1] By-products[cite: 1] Sporting Example[cite: 1]
ATP-PC (Alactic) Stored ATP (up to 3s) and Phosphocreatine (PCr)[cite: 1] 0 – 10 seconds | 1 ATP : 1 PC[cite: 1] None (Creatine and Phosphate reform slowly)[cite: 1] 100m sprint, shot put, weightlifting[cite: 1]
Lactate System (Anaerobic) Glycogen and Glucose broken down without oxygen[cite: 1] 10s – 60/90 seconds | 2/3 ATP[cite: 1] Lactic Acid (impedes contraction)[cite: 1] 400m sprint, 100m swim[cite: 1]
Aerobic System Glycogen, Fatty Acids (with oxygen)[cite: 1] 3+ mins to hours | 38 ATP total[cite: 1] Carbon Dioxide & Water[cite: 1] Marathon, cross-country skiing[cite: 1]
E5 & E6 Content

Energy System Adaptations & Energy Continuum

Energy Continuum Interplay

No energy system works in isolation; all three contribute simultaneously depending on exercise intensity and duration[cite: 1].

Progression: Stored ATP burns in 3s, PCr supplies 8-10s; Lactate system kicks in for mid-range (400m); Aerobic system takes over for endurance events[cite: 1].

Long-Term Training Adaptations
  • Increased Creatine Stores: improves ATP-PC system enabling longer high-intensity anaerobic work[cite: 1].
  • Increased tolerance to lactic acid and extended capillary networks to clear it faster[cite: 1].
  • Increased use of fats as energy source during long periods as glycogen depletes[cite: 1].
  • Increased glycogen storage and number/size of mitochondria increasing oxidative capacity[cite: 1].
